The Loop Current is South Florida's water heater

Caribbean water enters the Gulf through the 200-km-wide Yucatán Channel at speeds near 1.5–2 m/s. It swings north, loops clockwise, and leaves through the Florida Straits as the Florida Current, which carries roughly 30–32 million cubic metres per second (Sverdrups, Sv) past Miami before becoming the Gulf Stream.

Because that water arrives from the tropics, it keeps the Keys and southeast Florida mild in winter and stacks warm water deep below the surface year-round.

The cycle in the map above

  • Retracted: the current takes a short cut ("port to port") from Yucatán straight to the Straits.
  • Extended: it bulges north, sometimes past 27–28°N, close to the Louisiana and Florida shelves.
  • Shedding: the neck of the loop pinches off a warm-core eddy 200–400 km across, roughly every 3 to 18 months (about 8–11 on average).
  • Drift: the eddy spins clockwise and creeps west at a few kilometres a day, staying warm for months.

Why surface temperature hides it

Flip the map to Surface temp. In late summer nearly the whole Gulf sits at 29–31 °C, so satellites see almost nothing. Flip back to Heat below surface and the Loop and its eddies glow: warm water reaches 100–150 m down instead of 40–60 m.

That buried heat is measured as tropical cyclone heat potential (TCHP), the heat stored in water warmer than 26 °C. Above roughly 50–60 kJ/cm², a storm can churn the ocean without cooling its own fuel supply.

Reading the map

Moving streaks are water parcels; their speed is exaggerated for viewing. The red ring is a shed eddy. Pale areas are shallow banks and shelves, where there is simply not enough water to store much heat. The geometry is a teaching model, not a forecast.

Warm eddies don't start hurricanes. They feed them.

Hurricanes form from tropical disturbances, not from eddies. But once a storm exists, its winds stir the upper ocean. Over thin warm water, that stirring drags up cold water and the storm chokes its own fuel. Over a Loop Current eddy, the mixed water is still warm, so the storm can keep strengthening, sometimes rapidly.

Opal (1995) and Katrina and Rita (2005) intensified sharply over the Loop Current or its warm eddies; Michael (2018) also crossed unusually warm, deep Gulf water before landfall.

Coastal upwelling: cold water from below

Wind blowing along a coast does not push surface water straight downwind. Earth's rotation turns the net transport of the top tens of metres 90° to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere (Ekman transport). If that transport points offshore, deeper, colder, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it.

What it means here

  • Southeast Florida and the Keys: summer southerly and southeasterly winds, plus meanders and small frontal eddies on the edge of the Florida Current, can push water below 20 °C onto reefs off Miami and the Keys, a sudden summer chill divers notice.
  • West Florida Shelf: northerly winds drive upwelling along the Gulf coast. The Loop Current touching the shelf edge can also lift nutrients onto the shelf; one leading hypothesis links those nutrient pulses to the mix of phytoplankton, including the red tide alga Karenia brevis.
  • Reverse the wind and you get downwelling: warm surface water piles against the coast and the cold layer is pushed down.

More of South Florida's ocean

A slower current means higher water in Miami

A current this strong is balanced by a tilted sea surface: across the Straits, the sea stands lower on the Florida side than at the Bahamas. When the Florida Current weakens, the tilt relaxes and water rises along Miami's coast, even on a sunny day. Add a king tide and decades of sea-level rise, and low streets flood.

Florida Bay turns saltier than the sea

Florida Bay averages only 1–2 m deep and is cut off from the open ocean by mud banks and the Keys. In the dry season and in droughts, evaporation beats rain and Everglades inflow, and salinity climbs above seawater's 35. Past about 40 seagrass is stressed; in 1987 and 2015 hypersaline, hot, low-oxygen water helped kill tens of thousands of acres of turtle grass.

How hot is too hot for the Keys reef?

NOAA Coral Reef Watch tracks Degree Heating Weeks (DHW): every day the water is at least 1 °C above the warmest normal monthly mean adds heat stress, summed over 12 weeks. Around 4 DHW, bleaching is likely; at 8 and above, corals start dying. In summer 2023 the Florida Keys blew past those levels and saw mass bleaching.
