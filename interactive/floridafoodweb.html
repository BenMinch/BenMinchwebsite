Who eats whom off South Florida

A food web traces where the energy from sunlight goes. Pick a habitat, tap any species to see what it eats and what eats it, then remove or boost it and watch the ripple spread.

Reading a food web

  • Arrows point the way energy flows, from the thing eaten to the thing that eats it.
  • Producers (seagrass, algae, mangroves, phytoplankton) capture sunlight. Everything else runs on their energy.
  • Detritus is dead plant material coated in bacteria and fungi. In seagrass beds and mangroves, more energy passes through this "brown" web than through animals eating live plants.
  • Trophic level is how many steps a species sits above the producers. The web stacks species by it: producers at the bottom, top predators at the top.
  • Ripples (trophic cascades): removing a predator lets its prey increase, which then eat more of their food. Effects can jump two or three links.

Why there are so few sharks

Only a small share of the energy at one level becomes body mass at the next. The rest is burned for swimming, breathing and staying warm, or never eaten. Ecologists often use about 10% as a rule of thumb; real values run from roughly 5% to 20%.

The same stacking works in reverse for mercury. It is stored in tissue instead of being burned off, so it concentrates at each step. That is why Florida's fish-eating advisories focus on large predators such as sharks, king mackerel and big largemouth bass, not on mullet or shrimp.
